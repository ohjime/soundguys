Sound, chosen by the room

How cosound collects preference without a survey, how the server and the venue player fit together, and what it would take to predict a mixture worth listening to.

What cosound is

Cosound plays layered environmental audio in a shared public space and lets the people in that space steer it. A cosound is nothing more than a set of sound layers and a gain for each one. The research question underneath the product is narrow and hard: how does a public soundscape respond to a changing group of listeners without asking any of them to fill out a form?

The first venue is the public listening space at Cameron Library. The system runs a loop — a venue player renders the current mixture into the room, listeners react to it, the server folds those reactions into a new mixture, and the player fades to it. Everything in this write-up describes that loop as it is actually implemented today, and then argues about the one part of it that is still a placeholder: the choosing.

Where the design came from

The project began as a way to help Dr. Frishkopf collect listener feedback for curating a real-time public soundscape. The first mechanism was deliberately blunt: an upvote tag and a downvote tag on every table, written as NFC. Hearing something you like, you tap the upvote tag with your phone; hearing something you don't, you tap the other one. The tap opens a URL carrying the venue's player token, and the server identifies the listener from their session — if the phone isn't logged in, it asks them to log in before the vote counts.

That worked, and it collected almost nothing. A vote is one bit attached to a mixture; it tells you a person in that room at that moment approved or disapproved of a combination of four or five sounds, and gives you no way to know which of them did the work. The obvious fix — put a survey in front of the voter and build a proper listener profile — was rejected on purpose. It asks too much of somebody who came to the library to study, and it makes the room feel like a lab.

The alternative became the thing you are using right now. Give the listener a personal web mixer they actually want to use, and read the preference signal out of how they use it. They get a toy; the project gets a far richer record than any survey would have produced. Nobody is being measured against their will, because the measurement is the same act as the enjoyment.

Which raised the next problem: if the mixer is good enough to enjoy at home, why come back to the room and vote? The answer is the collection. The mixer is not stocked with the whole library — you can only build from sounds you have collected, and the way to collect a sound is to be in a room where it is playing and upvote it. Public listening becomes the way you acquire material for private listening, and each acquisition also happens to be a labelled preference event at a known place and time.

That loop is also the commercial argument, for whatever it turns out to be worth. Scarce, venue-exclusive soundscapes give a building a reason to want a player — foot traffic from people who want the sounds only that building plays. And soundscapes are neutral in a way music never is: a café that plays rock irritates one table and a café that plays rap irritates another, while rain on a window offends nobody.

Two sensors, two different signals

The system has exactly two ways of learning what a listener likes, and treating them as one signal would be a mistake. They measure different things, under different conditions, with different reliability.

The public vote The personal mixer
What it records Approval of a whole mixture, in a real room, at a known time and table Which sounds a listener chooses to combine, and at what relative levels
Resolution One bit over N layers — credit assignment is ambiguous Per-sound, per-gain, and repeatable
Context Everything: the room, the crowd, the HVAC, the hour None — headphones, unknown environment
Intent Judgement: "this fits here, now" Exploration: "what happens if I put these together?"
Cost to the listener One tap Minutes of deliberate play

The asymmetry matters for modelling. A saved mix is a strong statement about taste and a weak statement about fit — it was made in an unknown acoustic context, and the mixer is a playground, so a saved mix may be an experiment the listener enjoyed once and would never sit inside for an hour. A vote is the reverse: weak about taste, strong about fit. The public speaker is not only trying to predict what a person would like; it is trying to predict what works in that room for the people currently in it, which is a different target.

So the mixer signal probably wants to be discounted by recency and repetition — a sound that shows up across several saved mixes over weeks is taste, a sound that appears once at the top of a session is curiosity — while the vote signal wants to be weighted by context and treated as evidence about the combination, not the parts.

How the system works today

Three processes: the Django server (the only place decisions are made), the venue player (a read-only client that renders whatever the server says), and a scheduler loop that re-runs the prediction. The listener-facing web app is served by the same Django process.

The data model

Sound is a file plus a title, an artist, free-text tags, cover art, a short flavour line, and a pgvector embeddings column. Cosound is a mixture: a many-to-many to Sound through SoundLayer, which carries the gain. Every cosound is identified by two SHA-256 digests computed from its layers — hashid over sound ids and gains (gains quantised to the nearest 0.05 first), and hashset over the sound ids alone. That is what makes a mixture a first-class, deduplicated object: the same layers at the same levels are the same row no matter who built it, and hashset lets you ask "has this set of sounds ever been assembled, at any levels?"

A Listener wraps a user and owns a collection — the deck of sounds they are allowed to mix with. A Player is a venue endpoint: a manager, a name, a location, an assigned library (sounds), a 64-hex API token generated on first save, and playing, a pydantic Prediction stored as JSON that holds the layers currently assigned to that venue. A Vote joins a listener, a player, a cosound, an integer value, and the table's section string.

The vote path

  1. An NFC tag on a table encodes /vote/?player=<token>&section=<table>&choice=1 — or choice=0 for the downvote tag. Admin has a page that generates and copies these URLs per section.
  2. The page resolves the token to a player and builds a carousel of what is currently playing there: card zero is the venue, then one card per layer with its artwork, tags, gain and collection state.
  3. If the phone isn't authenticated, the vote is intercepted: a login card is pushed onto the deck, the intended partial is stashed in the session, and the vote resumes after passwordless email login — or as a guest account (@anon.cosound.ca), so a stranger can still vote.
  4. A per-listener throttle (60 seconds by default) rejects rapid repeat taps and reports the seconds remaining.
  5. The current layers are frozen into a Cosound via get_or_create_from_layers, and a Vote row is written against it. On an upvote, every sound in the mixture is added to that listener's collection — that is the "collect what you heard in public" mechanic, implemented in four lines.

One defect worth naming: the downvote tag sends choice=0 and the view stores that integer verbatim, while Vote.DOWNVOTE is defined as -1. Nothing consumes vote values yet, so nothing is currently wrong in production — but any scoring function that multiplies by value will silently treat every downvote as a no-op until the two agree.

The personal mixer

The mixer is a Web Audio engine driven by Alpine and htmx. It opens on three random library sounds, two of them audible, at random gains — deliberately unfinished, so the listener arrives with room to build rather than a mix that is already done. The cap is eight layers. Swapping or searching for a layer queries only the listener's collection, which is where the deck economy is enforced. Saving posts the layer JSON, clamps each gain into [0, 1], drops any layer whose id isn't a real Sound (blank slots and local files from the artist studio both carry browser-made ids), resolves the result to a Cosound, and attaches a titled SoundMix to the user.

The scheduler and the prediction

A management command loops forever: every 30 seconds it walks every Player and enqueues the configured predictor as a background task, resolved by dotted path from settings so the strategy is swappable without touching the loop. The predictor writes its result into player.playing. That is the entire decision surface of the system — one function, one player id, one Prediction out.

The player process

The venue player is a separate Python program with a Textual terminal UI, and it is strictly a client — it never decides anything.

  1. At startup it sends its token as X-API-Key and requests /manifest, receiving {sound_id: url} for its assigned library. It deletes local files no longer in the manifest and downloads the rest.
  2. Then it conditions every file once, offline, and caches the result keyed by a signature over size, mtime, target rate and target loudness: decode to float32, resample to the output device's native rate, apply a gentle high-shelf cut above ~13 kHz to tame lossy-codec fizz, crossfade the tail into the head so loops don't click, and normalise to −20 LUFS with a −1 dBFS peak ceiling. That last step is what lets layers sit together predictably — gains coming from the server mean something because every file arrives at the same loudness.
  3. Every 30 seconds it polls /player and compares a signature of the returned layers and gains against the last one. Unchanged, it does nothing. Changed, it queues each layer and triggers a transition.
  4. The transition is a gain ramp on the audio thread — new tracks ramp up from zero, dropped tracks ramp down to zero and are deleted, and files loop forever by modular indexing. Each new layer is assigned an azimuth from a van der Corput sequence, so that any number of active layers stays spread around the listener instead of piling onto one side.
  5. Rendering adapts to whatever the room has. The player probes the output device, infers a speaker layout from the channel count, and picks a mode: constant-power stereo panning on two channels, VBAP on a recognised 4/6/8-channel ring, and a geometry-free decorrelated upmix (flat-magnitude, random-phase all-pass per speaker) on anything unusual. A feedback-delay-network reverb adds a diffuse room field — vectorised in chunks no longer than the shortest delay line, so an inherently recursive structure still runs in numpy inside the audio callback.

The API is three read-only endpoints behind the token: /manifest, /player (venue details plus titles, artists and gains), and /cosound (the bare {sound_id: gain} map, kept for compatibility), all throttled to ten requests a minute.

What the recommender actually does right now

It is worth being exact about this, because the gap between it and the goal is the whole project. The current predictor:

  • treats a listener as present at a venue if they voted there in the last 30 minutes — a deliberately crude proxy for occupancy, and the one number most likely to change (the working definition has since drifted toward an hour);
  • counts every tag on every sound in every present listener's collection into a single Counter;
  • walks tag scores from highest to lowest and picks a random library sound carrying a tag at that score, falling back to a uniformly random library sound;
  • emits that one sound at gain 1.0 as the new mixture.

So the thing described as a layered adaptive soundscape is, at this moment, a tag-weighted random single-sound picker. The embedding column exists but is filled by random_sound_classifier, which returns five uniform random numbers — a placeholder wearing the shape of the real thing. Saved gains are recorded and never read. Nothing consumes vote polarity. And the candidate pool for saved-mix material is not restricted to the venue's assigned library, so a smarter version of this function could happily select a sound the player has never downloaded.

None of that is a criticism of the code — it closes the loop end-to-end, which is exactly what a baseline is for. But it means every interesting claim about preference learning is currently unearned, and the honest next step is not a bigger model. It is deciding what the model is supposed to be optimising.

The actual blocker: what is a good cosound?

Predicting mixtures is a search problem, and the search space is enormous. With a library of n sounds and up to eight layers, each with a continuous gain, there is no useful sense in which the system can explore it — and no ground truth to explore toward, because "good" here has never been defined. Votes give sparse, noisy, mixture-level feedback that would take an implausible amount of traffic to shape a search that large.

The temptation is to write down rules for what a good mixture is. That should be resisted, and mostly for a good reason: the interesting result is what listeners actually converge on, and a rulebook written in advance would just be the team's taste laundered through a recommender. The mixture level should stay loose and open, with minimal injected bias.

But "loose at the mixture level" only works if the search is constrained somewhere else — otherwise the system explores nothing but noise. The right place to put the structure is one level down, on the sounds. If every sound is described well enough, a large fraction of obviously-bad mixtures becomes unreachable without anyone having to legislate taste. Guardrails on the ingredients; freedom in the recipe.

Labelling the sounds properly

Today a sound might carry the tag "Rain". That is a start and not much more — it is one facet, chosen ad hoc, in a folksonomy inherited from wherever the file came from. Several established frameworks would give the same file far more usable structure, and they are complementary rather than competing.

1. Origin — the geophony / biophony / anthrophony split

Acoustic ecology's standard partition: geophony for non-biological natural sound (water, wind, thunder), biophony for non-human organisms (birds, insects), anthrophony for human-generated sound, itself split into controlled (music, speech) and uncontrolled (traffic, machine hum). Cheap to annotate, immediately useful as a diversity constraint — a mixture that is four biophony layers is a very different proposition from one geophony bed plus one biophony accent.

2. Function — Schafer's keynote / signal / soundmark

Class Role In a mix
Keynote Background that is rarely consciously heard but sets the character of a place The bed. You want roughly one, maybe two, and they can sit loud
Signal Foreground sound listened to consciously; carries information Accents. Too many and the room becomes a to-do list
Soundmark A sound a community treats as uniquely its own The venue-exclusive material the collection mechanic is built on

This one is arguably the highest-leverage label in the whole scheme, because it maps directly onto mixing rules. It is also the label most likely to need per-sound human judgement rather than a classifier.

3. Perception — the ISO 12913 circumplex

ISO 12913 codifies how people feel about an acoustic environment on two axes: pleasantness (unpleasant → pleasant) and eventfulness (uneventful → eventful), yielding four quadrants — calm (pleasant, uneventful), exciting (pleasant, eventful), chaotic (unpleasant, eventful), monotonous (unpleasant, uneventful). A library reading room is asking for the calm quadrant, and stating that as a coordinate rather than a vibe gives the recommender an actual objective.

There is a live methodological choice here between ISO 12913's eight-item questionnaire and SAM (Self-Assessment Manikin: valence, arousal, dominance). SAM is faster per stimulus, pictographic and language-independent, and its arousal axis maps more directly onto a calm objective than pleasantness alone — a vibrant carnival scores pleasant in ISO terms and is exactly what a library does not want. The pragmatic route is to collect SAM as the primary instrument and reconstruct approximate ISO pleasantness and eventfulness for comparability with the soundscape literature, calibrated on 30–50 stimuli rated on both instruments rather than asserted. SAM's dominance axis, usually discarded as redundant, may actually be worth keeping here: read as perceived intrusiveness, it captures the sound that feels like it is bearing down on you even at moderate arousal.

4. Clarity — hi-fi versus lo-fi

Schafer's other axis is essentially signal-to-noise. A hi-fi environment has depth — you can hear a distant bird and a nearby footstep at once. A lo-fi one is a wall of sound in which individual events are masked. This is a property a mixture acquires or loses through layering, which makes it a natural thing to predict from the layers and score against.

5. Raw acoustic description

None of the above tells you whether two files will physically sit together. That needs signal-level features, and they are the cheapest labels to obtain because they are computed, not judged: integrated loudness and loudness range, spectral centroid and band occupancy (where in the spectrum does this sound actually live), event density and stationarity (a continuous bed versus sparse discrete events), and periodicity. Masking predictions fall straight out of band occupancy: two sounds crowding the same octaves will fight, whatever their labels say.

This is also where the intuition about birds comes from. Stack several bright, high-frequency, event-dense biophony layers and the result is not "nature", it is being mobbed — high eventfulness, low pleasantness, the chaotic quadrant. A classifier that knows only "these are all bird sounds and the listener likes birds" will walk straight into it. One that also knows band occupancy and event density will not.

Public tags and hidden tags

Not every label needs to be shown. There are two audiences with genuinely different needs, and conflating them is what makes tag vocabularies rot. Public tags are for the listener browsing their deck in the mixer — evocative, sparse, curated, and allowed to be poetic. Hidden tags exist only for the recommender — band occupancy bucket, event density, keynote/signal role, provisional ISO coordinates, confidence flags, experimental labels being trialled. Hidden tags can be machine-written, revised, versioned and thrown away without ever confusing a person, which means the labelling scheme can stay experimental for as long as it needs to. The schema already supports this cheaply: taggit plus a namespace convention, or a separate model, and a Sound.public_tags accessor used by the templates.

Mixing rules the labels would buy

The point of labelling well is that the constraints become statements about ingredients, not about taste:

  • at most one or two keynote beds; signals capped by count, not by gain;
  • diversity across origin classes — penalise mixtures that are entirely biophony or entirely anthrophony;
  • spectral spread — penalise layers competing for the same band occupancy, which is the masking problem stated in features;
  • an event-density budget, so the total rate of discrete events stays in the uneventful half of the circumplex;
  • a gain budget, so that the sum of layers keeps headroom — the player already loudness-normalises every file, so gains are comparable across the library and this is arithmetic rather than guesswork.

Every one of those is a filter over candidate mixtures, not a prescription of the right answer. The space left over is still large, still open to exploration, and no longer full of mixtures nobody could stand.

Profiling listeners, and combining them

With sounds described in a shared feature space, a listener profile becomes a point (or a distribution) in that same space rather than a bag of tags. Collected sounds, saved-mix layers weighted by their gains, and upvotes each contribute, with different weights and different decay — and negative evidence finally becomes usable once downvotes stop being stored as zero.

Group selection is then its own problem with its own literature. Averaging profiles is the obvious move and the wrong one: the mean of two strong, opposed preferences is a mixture nobody asked for. The measures that matter are the trade-off between majority fit and worst-case individual dissatisfaction, and any aggregation rule has to be transparent enough to explain to a venue manager. Whatever is chosen must be evaluated against the current baseline, which is exactly what the baseline is there for.

What we would have to build, in order

  1. Fix the plumbing. Align downvote encoding with Vote.DOWNVOTE; restrict candidate sounds to the venue's assigned library; let a running player pick up a library change without a restart; settle the presence window at one number and use it consistently.
  2. Label the library. Annotate every sound on all five schemes — origin, function, perceptual coordinates, clarity, computed acoustics — splitting public tags from hidden ones. This is the unglamorous step everything else depends on.
  3. Replace the random embedding. Swap random_sound_classifier for a real one. The hook already exists as a settings path, and the pgvector column is already there; only the dimension needs to change.
  4. Model the mixture, not the sound. Score candidate cosounds against the labelled constraints, using gain-weighted mixture vectors rather than unweighted sets.
  5. Calibrate against people. Run a small rating study — SAM on standardised-length excerpts — to ground the perceptual coordinates and fit the ISO mapping, so claims about calmness are measured rather than asserted.
  6. Evaluate group selection. Compare aggregation rules against the baseline on vote outcomes, reporting both majority fit and worst-case dissatisfaction.

Reading list

Curated from the Freesound papers, datasets and projects indexes in docs/research. Ordered by how directly each item bears on the open problems above, not by date.

Closest prior art — read these first

  • Autonomous In-Situ Soundscape Augmentation via Joint Selection of Masker and Gain — Watcharasupat, Ooi, Lam et al., IEEE SPL 2022. Almost literally cosound's problem: choose which sound to add to a real environment and at what level, automatically, to improve perceived pleasantness. Paper
  • ARAUS: A Large-Scale Dataset and Baseline Models of Affective Responses to Augmented Urban Soundscapes — Ooi, Ong, Watcharasupat et al., IEEE TAC 2022. Human affective ratings of soundscapes augmented with added sounds at known gains — the labelled data our recommender does not have. Paper
  • A Framework for Computer-Assisted Sound Design Systems Supported by Modelling Affective and Perceptual Properties of Soundscape — Thorogood, Fan, Pasquier, 2019. Directly about generating soundscapes from modelled affect. See also the same group's Soundscape Generation Systems survey. Paper
  • Audio Metaphor: Audio Information Retrieval for Soundscape Composition — Thorogood, Pasquier, Eigenfeldt, 2012, and Computationally Created Soundscapes with Audio Metaphor, 2013. Retrieval-driven soundscape assembly from Freesound; the project is still live. Paper

Perceptual models and affect

  • A Comparison of Methods for Modeling Soundscape Dimensions Based on Different Datasets — Versümer, Blättermann, Rosenthal, Weinzierl, JASA 2025. What actually predicts pleasantness/eventfulness, and how much the answer depends on the dataset. Paper
  • An Exhaustive Variable Selection Study for Linear Models of Soundscape Emotions — San Millán-Castillo, Martino, Morgado, Llorente, IEEE/ACM TASLP 2022, and the 2024 decision-tree follow-up. Which acoustic features earn their place in an emotion model — directly relevant to picking our hidden tags. Paper
  • Soundscape Captioning Using Sound Affective Quality Network and Large Language Model — Hou, Ren, Mitchell, Wang, Kang et al., 2024. Joins acoustic scene, affective quality and description in one model. Paper
  • Singapore Soundscape Site Selection Survey (S5) — Ooi, Lam, Hong et al., Sustainability 2022. Clustering real sites into characteristic soundscapes; a template for describing what a venue is before deciding what to play there. Paper
  • Psychophysiological Responses of Young People to Soundscapes in Actual Rural and City Environments — Li, Cai, Dong, Wu, Chen, JAES 2021. Physiological rather than self-reported measures — worth knowing about before claiming anything about calm. Paper

Taxonomy and labelling

  • Heterogeneous Sound Classification with the Broad Sound Taxonomy and Dataset — Anastasopoulou, Torrey, Serra, Font, 2024. Freesound's own 23-class broad taxonomy, built for exactly our situation: a heterogeneous user-contributed library that needs organising. Paper
  • SALT: Standardized Audio Event Label Taxonomy — Stamatiadis, Olvera, Essid, 2024. Reconciling incompatible label sets across datasets. Paper
  • Ontology-Aware Learning and Evaluation for Audio Tagging — Liu, Kong, Liu, Mei, Wang, Plumbley, 2022. Why flat labels mislead evaluation when the label space is hierarchical. Paper
  • Facilitating the Manual Annotation of Sounds When Using Large Taxonomies — Favory, Fonseca, Font, Serra, 2018. Practical annotation tooling — read before hand-labelling the library. Paper
  • Improving Audio Retrieval Through Loudness Profile Categorization — Parekh, Font, Serra, 2016. Categorising sounds by how their loudness evolves — a sharp, cheap, mixing-relevant descriptor. Paper
  • Analysis of the Folksonomy of Freesound — Font, Serra, 2012, plus the tag-recommendation line: Folksonomy-Based Tag Recommendation (2013), Class-Based Tag Recommendation (2014) and its real-world impact study (2015). Since our sounds come from Freesound with Freesound tags, this is the analysis of the vocabulary we inherited. Paper

Representation and retrieval

  • CLAP: Learning Audio Concepts from Natural Language Supervision — Elizalde, Deshmukh, Al Ismail, Wang, 2022, and LAION-CLAP. The most plausible off-the-shelf replacement for the random embedding, and it gives text-queryable sounds for free. Paper
  • Transformation of Audio Embeddings into Interpretable, Concept-Based Representations — Zhang, Thomaz, Lu, 2025. Bridges "we need embeddings" and "we need to explain the choice to a venue manager". Paper
  • Semi-Supervised Triplet Loss Based Learning of Ambient Audio Embeddings — Turpault, Serizel, Vincent, 2019. Embeddings for ambient material specifically, with few labels. Paper
  • How Robust Are Audio Embeddings for Polyphonic Sound Event Tagging? — Abeßer, Grollmisch, Müller, 2023. Our target is inherently polyphonic — several layers at once — so robustness under overlap is the property that matters. Paper
  • A Semantic Hybrid Approach for Sound Recommendation — Ostuni, Di Noia, Di Sciascio, Oramas, Serra, 2015. Recommendation over a sound library rather than a music catalogue. Paper
  • The Language of Sound Search: Examining User Queries in Audio Search Engines — Weck, Font, 2024. How people actually ask for sounds — useful for choosing the public tag vocabulary. Paper

Datasets worth pulling

  • Emo-Soundscapes — 1,213 six-second clips with crowd-sourced valence/arousal ratings, and crucially 613 of them are mixtures of the 600 base recordings. Ratings on combinations, not just single sounds: the closest public analogue to what we are trying to predict. Dataset
  • SimSceneTVB (Perception and Learning) — 100 rated and 600 simulated 45-second urban scenes, each built from three controlled source classes (traffic, voices, birds) at known levels. A ready-made experiment in how source mix and level drive perception. Perception · Learning
  • BSD10k — 10,000+ Freesound clips labelled under the Broad Sound Taxonomy. The obvious training set if we adopt BST as the origin/function backbone. Dataset
  • FSD50K — 51,197 human-labelled Freesound clips over 200 AudioSet classes. The general-purpose tagging workhorse, same provenance as our library. Dataset
  • ESC-50 — 2,000 clips, 50 classes. Small enough to iterate on quickly; already the basis of the offline experiment in src/learn. Dataset
  • Freesound Loop Dataset — 9,455 loops with annotations. Our layers loop forever, so loop-specific material and its seam behaviour are directly relevant. Dataset

Standards and background

  • ISO 12913-1:2014 — the soundscape definition and conceptual framework the perceptual axes come from. PDF
  • Comparing Soundscape Assessment Methods of ISO 12913-2 (Methods A and B) with Narrative Interview (Method C) — the practical trade-offs between instruments, which is the decision behind choosing SAM. PDF
  • R. Murray Schafer, The Soundscape: Our Sonic Environment and the Tuning of the World — the origin of keynote / signal / soundmark and of hi-fi versus lo-fi. Not on the Freesound index; it is the source everything else cites.

Open questions

  • Should presence be a vote in the last hour, or something less gameable — and what happens to the mixture when the room empties?
  • How much should a saved mix count relative to a vote, given that one is exploration and the other is judgement?
  • Is the objective a fixed target in the calm quadrant, or does it move with time of day and occupancy?
  • What does the system do when the present listeners genuinely disagree — average, alternate, or optimise the worst case?
  • Do venue-exclusive soundscapes actually change behaviour, or is the collection mechanic only interesting to the people who built it?
